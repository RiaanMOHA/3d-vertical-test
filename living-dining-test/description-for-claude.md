# Living-dining (LDK) — photo description for prompt writers

This document describes the combined living and dining space on the ground floor of a two-storey Japanese house in the ozu-1 project. It is written for another Claude instance that will use it to author six photoreal Gemini Nano Banana prompts of the space from different camera angles. The description is built from the on-site photographs in the parent folder.

The room is one open volume. It is not two rooms. The "living" and "dining" labels are zones inside a single rectangular space with no internal partition between them, only a half-height kitchen counter that divides the kitchen zone from the LDK on the north side.

## Geometry and layout

The LDK is a rectangle, roughly six metres long by four-and-a-half metres deep, with a small carved-out corner in the north-west used by the staircase. Ceiling height reads as a normal Japanese residential 2.4 to 2.5 metres, so the room feels low and intimate rather than lofty.

Walls and their adjacencies:

- The long south wall is the garden wall. It is the main exterior wall and it carries two large sliding glass doors.
- Opposite the garden wall, on the long north side, is the kitchen wall. It is an interior wall. It contains a half-height kitchen pass-through counter and, at the west end, the entry to the corridor and the stair carve-out.
- The short east wall is the brick wall. It is an accent wall clad in dark faux brick across the full elevation.
- Opposite the brick wall, the short west wall is the stairs wall. The staircase shaft occupies its north half. The south half is plain.

The dining zone sits at the east end of the room, against the brick wall, next to the kitchen pass-through. The living zone (sofa, coffee table, television) sits at the west end, against the stairs wall and across from the staircase shaft. The balcony sliding doors are on the south wall, so they run along the whole length of the room between both zones.

A free-standing partition stub sticks out into the room from the kitchen wall, near the corridor entry. It is short, perhaps half the room depth, and it carries the intercom panel and the aircon remote holder. It is not a full dividing wall — it is more like an integrated console pillar that screens the corridor entrance from the dining zone view.

The staircase is in the north-west corner of the room. It runs along the stairs wall and ascends with a 90-degree turn. The first treads are visible from the LDK floor, set into a partition pocket: the bottom riser faces south, and the run turns east at a small landing roughly a third of the way up. The treads are pale unfinished oak with no risers shown, and the partition wall around the stair shaft is the same warm grey as the rest of the kitchen wall.

The kitchen pass-through is roughly two-and-a-half metres long, at counter height, with a pale natural-oak edged top. Behind it, in the kitchen zone proper, you can see a tall silver-grey two-door refrigerator and a brick-clad backsplash that matches the LDK brick wall in colour but is laid with lighter grout. The pass-through opens into the LDK from the centre-north of the kitchen wall.

## Surfaces

### Ceiling

The ceiling is clad in horizontal whitewashed wood planks running parallel to the brick wall — that is, east to west, the long axis of the room. The planks are heavily distressed: pale whitewash over warm pinewood, with dark brown knots, scrubbed-back grain and irregular weathered streaks bleeding through. It reads as a French-farmhouse shabby-chic finish, not as a Japanese natural-wood ceiling. White-painted crown moulding runs around the perimeter where the ceiling meets the wall. Small recessed circular downlights are punched into the planks at intervals, and a small round white smoke detector sits flat against the ceiling somewhere near centre.

### Walls

The kitchen wall, the garden wall and the stairs wall all share the same finish: a warm neutral grey textured wallpaper with a faint matte sandy granularity. The grey is a slightly warm beige-grey, not cold blue-grey. Skirting boards along the bottom are slim, white-painted, with a simple rectangular profile. There is no crown moulding on the walls themselves; the wall meets the ceiling planks via the white perimeter trim already mentioned.

The brick wall is the only accent surface. It is faux brick veneer in a stretcher bond, with bricks that read as dark chocolate-brown shading into reddish-brown and occasional darker charcoal bricks for variation. The grout is a light cream-grey, drawn slim. The texture is rough and matte. The brick covers the full short east wall from floor to ceiling, edge to edge.

### Floor

The floor is light oak laminate plank with a satin sheen, planks running east to west (parallel to the garden wall). The tone is pale honey-cream with very light brown grain streaks — much paler than the brick or the dining table, and pale enough that the room feels light despite the warm walls and dark ceiling planks.

Door frames are white-painted to match the skirting.

## Windows and doors

The south garden wall holds two large aluminium-framed sliding glass doors, fitted side by side and reading almost like one continuous glazed elevation. The frames are slim and white. The doors run from very low (small skirting only) to ceiling height. Each door is dressed with a pair of cream-beige opaque curtains in a smooth slightly-sheen drape fabric, with a separate white sheer curtain layered behind them. The opaque cream curtains are pulled to the sides and held with matching cream fabric tiebacks. The sheers are usually drawn closed. Through the glass you can see neighbouring tile-roofed houses across a narrow gap — this is a suburban Japanese setting.

The brick wall holds one tall vertical rectangular window cut into the brick, roughly mid-wall horizontally, with the sill about one metre off the floor and the head close to the ceiling. The frame is white. It carries the same cream curtain + white sheer + tieback set as the garden wall, in a smaller proportion. Through it you glimpse the neighbouring building's wall and roof tiles.

The kitchen wall has a small tall narrow frosted-glass window above the dining zone end, near the chandelier. White frame, frosted glass, no curtain — used as a daylight slit.

The stairs wall has a similar very tall narrow frosted vertical slit window, glowing bright but obscured, lighting the stair shaft area and the partition between LDK and corridor.

The corridor entry on the kitchen wall is a single hinged white door panel inset with a tall vertical strip of smoked dark glass. The handle is a slim metal lever in matte silver. The door colour matches the white skirting and trim.

## Furniture

### Living zone

The sofa is a two-seater mid-century Japanese piece, in the family of Karimoku 60 / vintage Showa lounge sofas. The upholstery is dark espresso-brown leather with button tufting on the seat back, and the frame is exposed solid wood in a warm walnut-toned brown, with splayed tapered legs and gently curved armrests that drop from a higher back to lower armrests. The seat cushion is a single bench cushion, not split. The sofa sits roughly in the centre-west of the room, facing east-north-east toward the television.

The coffee table is low, rectangular, with a dark cherry / walnut-stained timber top showing visible long grain. The frame is matte black slim steel, and there is a lower secondary shelf in dark expanded-metal mesh. Industrial-rustic in style. A small clear glass vase with a few small pink flowers sits on the top.

The television stand is the same industrial-rustic family as the coffee table: a black steel frame, a reddish-brown timber top, and four small drawers across the front with simple black metal pulls. It is wider than the television and sits flush against the kitchen wall, between the corridor partition stub and the kitchen pass-through. On top of it stands a large flat-screen television, around 50 to 55 inches, freestanding on its own central base — not wall mounted. The screen is glossy black.

### Dining zone

The dining table is a rectangular six-seater (sometimes laid for four) with a striking burnt-cedar / yakisugi-style top: deep bold caramel-amber grain with very prominent darker grain bands and warm orange highlights. The grain runs along the long axis of the table. The legs are matte black slim hairpin or square-tube metal legs. The vibe is industrial-craft, not traditional joinery.

The dining chairs are mismatched on purpose. Across the photos there are typically four to five chairs grouped at the table, in a deliberately eclectic mix: a navy / dark blue molded plastic shell chair with black hairpin legs; a charcoal-black molded shell chair with black legs; a tan / mustard moulded shell chair; a dark green moulded shell chair; and a leather-upholstered chair in a deep oxblood-burgundy with black metal legs. All chairs share the same matte black metal leg family so they read as a curated set despite the colour mix.

### Decor

A clear glass bottle vase with a single sprig of dried lavender (pale violet flower head) sits on the dining table centre. Decorative items are otherwise minimal — no books, no clutter.

## Light fixtures

Two near-identical chandeliers hang from the ceiling, one over the dining table and one over the coffee table / living area. Each has a dark wrought-iron scrollwork body with four arms curving outward and upward, ending in upward-facing opaque white bell-shaped frosted-glass shades. The bell shades have a slight ruffle at the rim. The chandelier over the dining area is slightly larger / longer-armed than the living-area one, but they belong to the same family.

The antique wall clock is the standout decorative fixture. It is a double-faced "station clock" hanging on a heavy wrought-iron scrolled bracket that projects horizontally out from the kitchen-wall corner near where the brick wall meets it. The clock face is cream with black Roman or Arabic numerals (legibly something like "London Bridge Station Platform"), surrounded by a black metal bezel ring, with a fleur-de-lis decorative finial on top of the bracket. It sits high on the wall, just under the ceiling, and is visible from both the dining zone and the living zone. Heavy industrial-vintage aesthetic.

Small recessed circular downlights are sprinkled across the ceiling between and around the chandeliers — modest size, warm-white LEDs.

## Fixtures and fittings on walls

The aircon unit is a horizontal white plastic split-unit mounted high on the brick wall, very close to the brick-wall–kitchen-wall corner (the north-east corner of the LDK). It is positioned just below ceiling height, sitting clearly on top of the brick surface. Brand is not clearly visible but it reads as a Japanese domestic-style unit (Toshiba per the room map).

The intercom panel is white, with a small rectangular video screen and a couple of buttons, mounted on the free-standing partition stub that faces the living zone. Directly below or beside it sits a small wall cradle holding a white aircon remote controller. A double rocker light switch (also white plastic) is mounted on the same partition next to or below the intercom.

Power outlets — white plastic duplex receptacles — are visible near the skirting in several places, including beside the dining table and on the corridor partition. Nothing brass or metallic.

There is no visible duct work or vents beyond the AC head.

## Lighting and atmosphere in the photos

The photographs read as bright daytime, taken on a clear day. Strong cool-neutral daylight enters from the south sliding doors and floods the floor in long bright bands; smaller bright slots of daylight come in through the brick-wall window and the two frosted slits. Sky visible through the brick-wall window is pale blue.

The chandeliers are switched on in most photos, so the room mixes two colour temperatures: cool 5500K-ish daylight at the windows, and warm ~2700K incandescent / warm-LED glow from the chandeliers in the central volume and over the dining and coffee tables. The warm artificial light bounces off the brick wall and the timber tabletops, giving the inside of the room a warm honey cast even in the middle of the day. The overall feel is bright, calm, slightly cafe-like, with the contrast between cool light at the perimeter and warm light at the centre.

There are no curtains drawn fully shut and no shadows so soft they suggest overcast — direct sun is reaching the floor, suggesting late morning to early afternoon on a clear day.

## Decorative items recap

- Dining table: single clear glass bottle vase with dried lavender sprig (violet-purple).
- Coffee table: small clear glass vase with a few small pink flowers.
- Wall: the antique double-faced station-style clock on its wrought-iron bracket.
- No books, art, posters, rugs, or wall hangings beyond the clock.

## Worth noting / quirks

- The mixed styling is deliberate. The room blends French-rustic shabby-chic ceiling planks, dark industrial faux brick, mid-century Japanese leather-and-walnut lounge furniture, industrial-craft black-metal-and-timber dining and TV pieces, a yakisugi-style table top, mismatched designer plastic shell chairs, twin wrought-iron bell chandeliers, and a Victorian-railway-style double-faced wall clock. It should not be rendered as a "clean modern Japanese" room — it is intentionally eclectic and warm.
- The ceiling planks are NOT raw natural wood. They are whitewashed with knots and weathered streaks. Avoid prompts that imply a clean blonde unfinished pine ceiling.
- The dining table top is unusually high-contrast and grain-heavy — almost a feature object in its own right.
- There is no rug. The pale oak floor reads cleanly throughout.
- The sofa always faces north-east-ish toward the TV stand against the kitchen wall, and the TV is in line with the corridor partition stub.
- The two chandeliers are slightly different sizes but belong to the same wrought-iron-and-frosted-bell family.
- The aircon sits on the brick, not the grey wall. This is a deliberate accent placement.
- The clock projects off the wall on a bracket. It is not flat-mounted. Prompts should specify the wrought-iron scroll bracket.
- The staircase is L-shaped (turns 90 degrees), not straight or U-shaped. The bottom run faces south then turns east.
- The garden-wall sliding doors are paired and span almost the full south wall — they are the dominant light source and should be unobstructed in any south-facing render.
- The kitchen pass-through is half-height, with a pale natural oak top edge — not full height, not a wall.
- Both curtain sets (south wall and east brick-wall window) use the same cream opaque + white sheer + cream tieback combination, for visual continuity.
